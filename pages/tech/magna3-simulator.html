Change the control mode, close the valves, switch to glycol, starve the inlet until it cavitates or pull it apart. Speed, flow, head, power and temperature all come from one physics model, so the impeller, the water, the charts and the pump's display always agree.

What's inside

Wet-rotor PM motor

The rotor carries permanent magnets and spins inside a thin stainless rotor can, in the heating water itself. No shaft seal, so nothing can leak. The water also cools and lubricates the bearings, and the pump can run against closed valves for days.

Composite impeller and volute

Water rises from the lower flange, loops behind the pump into the eye of the impeller, is flung outward by seven backward-curved blades and leaves through the upper flange. Pressure builds along the way and the particles turn from blue to red. The eye is where pressure is lowest, so that's where cavitation starts.

AUTOADAPT

The factory setting. It's proportional pressure from a fixed 1.5 m base, starting at about 55% of max head. When the pump finds itself on its max curve at a lower head, it picks a lower curve on its own. Here that learning is sped up from hours to seconds.

Limits you can hit

Between about 5 and 20 m³/h the pump reaches its 333 W input limit before top speed. That limit is computed, not drawn, so denser or more viscous liquids pull the max curve down. FLOW LIMIT caps the flow between 25 % and 90 % of max. Hot water needs inlet pressure: 0.7 bar at 110 °C.

How it was built

Built with the teardown method: real numbers first, one physics function that drives every part and gauge, and an idle auto tour that takes over after 25 seconds.

The outer shell is Grundfos's own CAD model of product 97924259 (housing, motor head and control box), turned into a web mesh and recoloured to match the product photo. The head and power curves are fitted to the official performance chart, and the 333 W limit falls out of the power model rather than being drawn on. Control behaviour, automatic night setback, inlet pressures and glycol limits follow the MAGNA3 installation and operating instructions.

On top of that sit standard engineering methods: the EN 16297 energy efficiency index (it comes out at 0.168 against the label's ≤ 0.18, a useful check on the fit), the ANSI/HI 9.6.7 viscosity correction for glycol, Euler's pump equation with Wiesner slip at the impeller, NPSH from vapour pressure and inlet pressure, and EN 442 radiator output in the heating-season comparison. Liquid properties come from standard fits for water and ASHRAE tables for ethylene glycol.

The internals (impeller, rotor, can, stator) and the split of losses between converter, motor and hydraulics aren't public, so they're estimates sized to agree with the official curves. This is an illustration, not a selection tool: use Grundfos Product Center for real sizing.
